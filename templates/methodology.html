Engine Methodology

The AMG Options Pricing Engine bridges the gap between academic theory and real-world trading. It uses the standard Black-Scholes-Merton model for quick theoretical checks, and a heavy-duty Monte Carlo simulation to account for how volatility actually behaves in live markets.

1. Black-Scholes-Merton (The Intuition)

Black-Scholes-Merton (BSM) is the industry standard for pricing options. While the math looks intimidating, it is essentially just an expected value calculation. It calculates what the option payout will be at expiration, multiplied by the probability of it finishing "in-the-money", and discounts that cash back to today.

$$ C = S_0 e^{-qT} N(d_1) - K e^{-rT} N(d_2) $$

Think of $N(d_1)$ as the Delta (how much the option acts like the underlying stock), and $N(d_2)$ as the literal probability that the option finishes in the money.

How The 5 Inputs Drive the Price
1. Spot Price ($S_0$)

Logic: If you hold a Call option (the right to buy), a higher current stock price makes your locked-in strike price much more valuable.

2. Strike Price ($K$)

Logic: A higher strike price makes a Call option cheaper. Why? Because you have to pay more money to exercise your right to buy the stock.

3. Time to Expiration ($T$)

Logic: More time = higher option value. Options are insurance policies. The longer the policy lasts, the more time there is for the stock to make a massive, profitable move.

4. Implied Volatility ($\sigma$)

Logic: Options have capped downside (you can only lose your premium) but unlimited upside. High volatility means wild swings. Because your losses are capped but your gains aren't, wilder swings make the option significantly more valuable.

5. Risk-Free Rate ($r$)

Logic: Buying a Call option is essentially a leveraged stock purchase. You get exposure to the stock today, but don't have to pay the strike price until expiration. If interest rates are high, keeping that cash in the bank to earn interest is very valuable. Therefore, higher rates make Call options slightly more expensive.

2. GARCH (Real-World Volatility)

The biggest flaw in the BSM model is that it assumes volatility ($\sigma$) is constant over time. Anyone who has traded knows this is false. Markets experience volatility clustering: calm days are followed by calm days, and massive crashes are followed by massive aftershocks.

$$ \sigma_t^2 = \omega + \alpha \epsilon_{t-1}^2 + \beta \sigma_{t-1}^2 $$

We fix the BSM flaw by using the GARCH model. GARCH dynamically updates the volatility forecast every single day by looking at three things: the long-term average ($\omega$), yesterday's market shock ($\alpha$), and yesterday's variance ($\beta$). If the market tanks today, GARCH automatically spikes the volatility for tomorrow.

3. Monte Carlo Simulation

Because GARCH means our volatility is changing every single day, we can no longer use a simple, closed-form formula like BSM. Instead, we use computational brute force.

The engine runs a Monte Carlo simulation. It generates 10,000 distinct, random future paths for the asset's price between today and expiration. At every single daily step, it injects the shifting GARCH volatility to determine how wildly the stock might move.

By calculating the exact payout of your multi-leg strategy across all 10,000 alternate realities and averaging the results, we generate a highly accurate, institution-grade theoretical price that accounts for dynamic market conditions.
